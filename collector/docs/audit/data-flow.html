Station 1 & 2 — who is asked, and what they will answer

Uber alone takes four collectors, because Uber is four unrelated surfaces wearing one name: a CSV report pipeline, a supplier GraphQL endpoint, a chronicle timeline and an OAuth REST API. Each has its own auth, its own maximum window and its own memory. Pick a provider to follow it end to end.

The order is not alphabetical — it is by outstanding need

Eight sources run inside one window, sequentially, behind a queue that admits exactly one collection at a time. FMS used to go first. An FMS year backfill walks 130 vehicles through twelve monthly windows and takes four and a half hours; this deployment restarts the container on every push, and a restart requeues the running job from the top. So the backfill re-ran four hours of FMS on every deploy and reached Uber on none of them — while the 299-day Uber hole it existed to fill survived weeks of attempts, each reporting no failure at all.

Cheapest and most-broken first, then. A collector_checkpoint row is written the moment a window finishes, so the next attempt continues rather than restarts — marked after the write, never before, because every collector write is an upsert and a lost checkpoint costs one repeated window and never correctness.

Station 4 · four folds, four different kinds of collision

Where two records become one

This is the whole of the work. Nothing else in the pipeline is hard: the collectors ask and store. These four rules decide what counts as one trip, one person, one car and one day — and each of them was written after the wrong answer had already shipped.

The twin — one physical journey, two feeds

The FMS collector derives a journey from GPS whenever a vehicle moves. When a driver completes an Uber ride, both sources record it.

sql/schema_v7.sql — checked on one plate for one day: every Uber trip has an FMS twin two to five minutes later with near-identical distance. Summing them counted one physical journey twice, so the fleet's headline trip count and total distance were both roughly double the truth. trip_norm.is_booking = platform <> 'fms' separates the two populations at the view, once, rather than in the nineteen queries that had each rediscovered it. Telematics rows are not discarded — they are the only thing that can catch movement with no booking behind it.

The person — no provider shares an id with any other

Uber issues a UUID, Yango a different id, Bolt a third, the hotel channel a fourth. Counting DISTINCT driver_ext_id counts records, not people.

api/custody_sql.js:117 — personFold is two nested regexes: lower, collapse whitespace, btrim, then collapse an immediately repeated word — '(\m\w+)( \1)+' → '\1'. It is byte-for-byte the CANON helper in api/server.js, and test/consistency.test.mjs fails if the two strings ever differ, so the vehicle page and the driver page cannot disagree about how many people there are. Live over the last 90 days: 18 of 145 names carry a repeated surname, and 5 people hold two platform accounts each. Computing the fold per row cost /api/alerts/by-driver 93 seconds over the whole record, so it is now a STORED generated column on trip, driver_statement_day and four other tables — the same expression, asserted identical by test/person_key.test.mjs.

The week — a report spread over its days, finest window wins

driver_performance is a log of report windows, and its key is the window. It was never a set of disjoint periods, and every query that summed it assumed it was.

sql/schema_v23.sql — driver_payout_day_live. Every period is spread evenly across the days it covers; each day then takes exactly one period — the SHORTEST one covering it, ties broken by whichever was ingested most recently. No day is counted twice and no day is dropped. Even spreading is an estimate and the product says so: a driver earns nothing on a rest day. The seam above is not a provider boundary but our own grid: EARNER_DAY_HORIZON = 200 in src/sources/uber.js, and 2026-09-02 minus 200 days is 2026-02-14 — so 15 February took its own daily measurement while the six days before it kept their seventh of a week. Computing this view live cost a one-vCPU database twenty seconds a query, so driver_payout_day is a table refreshed by src/rollup.js in one DELETE+INSERT transaction; readers never see a half-built answer.

Retention · measured live, 2026-09-02

Every surface forgets at a different rate. That is why pages have blanks.

Uber's trip reports go back years. Uber's money is a rolling window of about 192 days that moves forward every night, so a day that was never collected inside it can never be collected at all. These two facts live behind one provider name, and confusing them is how a fleet concludes it had a bad February.

The earner-payments horizon, probed live this morning

Three identical requests to getEarnerBreakdownsV2, one week apart from each other, ten drivers each. Only the date changed.

2025-04-05 · first trip on record · 516 days of trips
◂ 2026-02-22 · the money edge, as at today
09–16 Feb → 0 rows
16–23 Feb → 10 rows, 7 with money
24–31 Aug → 10 rows, 5 with money

The edge rolls forward daily, so the collector's grid deliberately overshoots it — EARNER_DAY_HORIZON = 200, because overshooting costs a few empty calls and undershooting loses days that can never be re-fetched. Before this bound existed, a two-year backfill spent 73 of 100 weekly windows per fleet asking Uber for something it will never serve, at 72 seconds a window — roughly three and a half hours of every backfill, every week, spent being told nothing, while FMS waited behind it with a 73-day alert hole.

A blank is not always a gap

Four different things render as an empty cell, and the product is required to tell them apart — because three of them are facts about the world and one is a bug.

Never asked

No collector calls this surface. Not a gap — a question nobody has put.

ACCEPTANCE was an em-dash on every driver's Quality tab for the product's whole life. Not a missing column: REPORT_TYPE_DRIVER_QUALITY was a report never requested.

Asked, answered empty

The call succeeded and the provider had nothing. A genuinely quiet day.

Recorded per window in collection_run.detail with the driver count beside it — "10 of 85 drivers answered" is not a quiet week, and only that number tells the two apart.

Window refused

The request was too wide, or the credential was declined. Retryable.

FMS answers HTTP 400 above ~11,026 alert records and HTTP 200 with {"error":"Authentication failed"} for a bad password. A 400 must never reach the credential banner; a 200 must.

No longer held

Past the provider's retention. Permanent — asking again returns nothing, for ever.
